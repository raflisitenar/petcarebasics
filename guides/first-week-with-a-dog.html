Getting started / Field guide 01

The first seven days with a new dog

Set up calm routines, learn your dog’s signals, and avoid overwhelming the first week.

By PetCare Basics Editorial · Updated 6 November 2026 · Educational guidance
A practical reading guide, not a diagnosis or treatment plan. Individual pets and local conditions differ.

Prepare a predictable home base

Before the dog arrives, choose a quiet resting area that is not in the middle of a busy walkway. Put water, washable bedding and a safe resting option there. Check doors, balconies, stairs, cords, cleaning supplies and anything small enough to swallow. Place a collar or harness and suitable lead near the door so exiting the home does not turn into a scramble. If a crate is part of your plan, introduce it positively rather than using it as punishment. The aim is a place the dog can retreat to, not a place where every visitor immediately reaches in to greet them.

Make day one deliberately boring

A new home is full of unfamiliar sounds, smells and expectations. Offer a quiet arrival, access to water, a known or veterinarian-approved diet and regular opportunities to toilet. Resist inviting the whole family or neighborhood to meet the new dog immediately. Watch from a comfortable distance: does the dog choose to explore, rest, hide or approach? Respect those choices. A dog who is not ready for petting or a long walk can still have a successful first day. Avoid intense exercise or crowded venues while you are learning the animal’s health and temperament.

Use consistent toileting opportunities

A predictable schedule helps the household notice patterns, even if the dog is already toilet-trained. Offer a suitable toileting location after waking, meals and other transitions, and calmly reward appropriate elimination. Clean accidents thoroughly without scolding; punishment can create fear without teaching the desired location. Note any unusual straining, discomfort, frequency or absence of elimination and contact a veterinarian if there is concern. Puppies and older dogs may need different arrangements. A written handover between family members prevents missed opportunities and conflicting expectations.

Start training with easy wins

Choose two or three everyday cues such as responding to a name, coming toward you indoors and settling calmly on a mat. Keep sessions short, reward the behavior you want and reduce distractions before adding difficulty. Do not test a new dog by placing them in a crowded dog park to see what happens. Social experiences should be gradual and suited to health status and comfort. The goal is not to force the dog to be friendly with everything. It is to build an understandable relationship and provide situations where they can succeed.

Arrange a veterinary handover

Collect vaccination history, parasite-prevention records, medications, previous concerns and any relevant shelter or breeder documents. Arrange an initial veterinary appointment on a timeline suitable for the dog and ask about local risks rather than assuming an online schedule applies everywhere. Keep a copy of the current clinic’s contact details and an after-hours emergency option. Ask how to transition food, identify body-condition concerns and approach exercise given age and medical history. If the dog is unwell, do not wait for a routine appointment.

Review the pattern at the end of each day

Write down the practical essentials: how the dog ate, drank, rested, toileted, moved and responded to ordinary household activity. A short record is more useful than trying to remember every moment. It lets you notice gradual improvement or a concerning change. Share the same rules with everybody in the home, including when the dog can rest undisturbed. By day seven, decide which routines are working and which still create stress, then adapt gradually rather than starting over with a completely different system.

REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE

How this could look at home

A newly adopted dog refuses visitors on the first evening but eats, drinks and rests in the quieter part of the house. Instead of arranging more introductions, the household moves greetings to another week, keeps walks short and observes comfort. If appetite, behavior or toileting are concerning, they contact the veterinarian rather than assuming it is only adjustment.

Takeaway checklist

  • Choose a quiet sleeping area and check exits.
  • Write a shared feeding, toileting and walk schedule.
  • Collect medical records and the emergency clinic contact.
  • Schedule a veterinary check based on the dog’s circumstances.
