How to welcome a new cat into your home

A calm first-room approach helps a cat learn where safety, food and toilets are.

By PetCare Basics Editorial · Updated 6 November 2026 · Educational guidance
A practical reading guide, not a diagnosis or treatment plan. Individual pets and local conditions differ.

Begin with one comfortable room

Choose a quiet room with food and water stations, a litter box, a hiding place, scratching surface and a comfortable rest area. Keep litter away from food and give the cat a way to move between essential resources without feeling trapped. A new cat does not need immediate access to the entire house. Make sure windows, balcony doors, screens and cupboards are secure. If there are existing pets, arrange introductions separately and slowly. The initial goal is confidence and predictability, not a perfect social-media reunion.

Let the cat choose contact

Place the carrier on the floor, open it and allow the cat to emerge when ready. Avoid reaching into a hiding space or repeatedly moving the cat toward visitors. Quiet conversation and predictable movements are usually more useful than forced cuddling. Observe whether the cat eats, drinks, uses the litter box and begins to explore. Cats may hide when overwhelmed, but prolonged loss of appetite, urinary difficulty or other concerning signs should prompt veterinary advice rather than an assumption that all hiding is normal.

Make the litter setup straightforward

Choose a box the cat can enter comfortably and a location that is easy to find, quiet and accessible. Start with a familiar litter if that information is available. Scoop regularly and watch for differences in urine or stool. A cat that avoids the box may be reacting to access, litter preference, household stress or a medical problem; do not punish them. Any straining or inability to pass urine is an emergency and needs immediate veterinary care. If more than one cat lives in the home, distribute resources to reduce competition.

Offer choice and vertical space

Cats benefit from safe hiding, scratching, resting, climbing and opportunities for predatory-style play. Place a scratcher near an area the cat naturally uses rather than hiding it behind a sofa. Provide a perch or secure vertical access where practical. Interactive play can involve a wand toy followed by a small food opportunity if appropriate. Avoid leaving string or ribbon toys unattended. A quiet and enriched space is more valuable than filling the room with expensive accessories the cat cannot use comfortably.

Plan healthcare before problems

Bring adoption papers, known vaccination history, diet, identification and medications to the first veterinary visit. Discuss parasite prevention, sterilization where appropriate, feeding, dental health, vaccination and whether the cat has any special needs. The veterinarian can advise what is important for this cat, at this life stage, in your location. Keep the cat’s carrier accessible and introduce it as an everyday positive space so future appointments are less stressful. Do not give human medicines without veterinary instruction.

Expand territory in stages

Once the cat is confidently using essential resources and moving around the initial room, open additional spaces gradually while maintaining access to familiar resources. Watch for blocked routes or conflicts if other animals live there. Maintain predictable feeding, rest and play routines. The rate of expansion is determined by behavior rather than a fixed number of days. If the cat appears overwhelmed, reduce the area again and make the environment easier to navigate. Keep a short observation log so you know which changes helped.

REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE

How this could look at home

A shy cat remains under a chair for much of the first evening. Instead of pulling it out, the owner provides water, litter, a hiding box and a calm schedule, then monitors eating and litter use. The owner asks a veterinarian promptly about persistent refusal to eat or urinary problems.

Takeaway checklist

  • Prepare one quiet room and secure windows.
  • Separate litter, water and feeding areas.
  • Arrange veterinary records and carrier practice.
  • Move introductions at the cat’s pace.
