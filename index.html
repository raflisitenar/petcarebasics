Everyday dog & cat care

A calmer guide to caring for your pet.

Practical routines for feeding, grooming, enrichment, home safety and preparation—written for ordinary dog and cat owners who want to understand the basics.

General guidance, not diagnosis

Every animal is different. Individual nutrition, medication, vaccination, illness and treatment decisions should be discussed with a qualified veterinarian who knows the pet.

01 — Everyday foundations

Build a routine you can actually maintain.

Consistent observation is one of the most useful habits in pet ownership. A familiar routine makes it easier to notice when appetite, activity, toileting, mobility, coat condition or behavior changes.

Food & water

Provide fresh water and an appropriate diet in portions suited to the individual animal. Avoid assuming that a serving suitable for one pet is automatically suitable for another.

Movement

Dogs and cats both benefit from opportunities to move, explore and engage. The appropriate amount and intensity depends on age, health, environment and temperament.

Enrichment

Play, scent work, scratching opportunities, training and food puzzles can help provide mental engagement when selected appropriately for the pet.

Grooming

Coat, nails, ears and oral care deserve regular attention. Grooming needs differ substantially by coat type, species and individual condition.

Rest & retreat

Provide a comfortable area where the animal can rest without constant interruption. Cats in particular often benefit from access to safe hiding and elevated spaces.

Observe changes

Learn what is normal for your pet. Persistent or concerning changes in eating, drinking, breathing, elimination, mobility or behavior deserve professional attention.

    02 — Safer surroundings

    Look at the home from your pet's level.

    Everyday hazards to manage

    • Secure medications, household chemicals and pest-control products.
    • Keep potentially hazardous foods and plants inaccessible.
    • Manage cords, strings, small swallowable objects and unsecured trash.
    • Use secure screens, doors, gates and transport equipment appropriate to the animal.
    • Store human and veterinary medications according to instructions.

    Prepare before a problem

    • Keep veterinarian and emergency clinic details accessible.
    • Maintain identification appropriate to your pet and local requirements.
    • Have a safe carrier or restraint method available.
    • Keep important health and medication information organized.
    • Plan who can care for the pet if you are unexpectedly unavailable.
    Urgent concerns: difficulty breathing, collapse, severe trauma, uncontrolled bleeding, seizures, suspected poisoning, severe distress or other rapidly worsening symptoms warrant prompt veterinary attention. Do not use a website to delay emergency care.
    03 — Life stages

    Care priorities change over time.

    Age labels are broad categories rather than precise medical definitions. Breed, body size, history and individual health all affect when needs change.

    04 — New pet checklist

    Before the first day home.

    Prepare one calm area

    Set up food/water, resting space, toileting arrangements and species-appropriate essentials before arrival instead of introducing the entire home at once.

    Plan professional care

    Know which veterinary clinic you intend to use and organize any existing records, medication information and previous care history.

    Agree on household rules

    Decide who handles feeding, walks, litter care, training and supervision so routines are consistent from the beginning.

    05 — Travel preparation

    Make the trip boring—in a good way.

    Good travel preparation reduces last-minute improvisation. Requirements vary by destination and mode of transport, so confirm current carrier, accommodation and regulatory rules before travel.

    Pack & verify

    • Secure carrier or appropriate restraint.
    • Identification and contact details.
    • Food, water and necessary routine supplies.
    • Required records and prescribed medications.
    • Destination veterinary/emergency information for longer trips.

    Before departure

    • Confirm pet policies and transport requirements.
    • Allow the pet to become familiar with carriers gradually when possible.
    • Keep medications in original/labeled packaging where appropriate.
    • Avoid introducing unnecessary diet changes immediately before travel.
    Reference

    Pet-care glossary.

    Body conditionA structured way of assessing whether an animal appears underweight, ideal or overweight based on body shape and palpable fat coverage.
    EnrichmentActivities or environmental features that encourage appropriate mental and physical engagement.
    MicrochipA small identification device implanted under the skin; registration details need to remain current for it to be useful.
    Preventive careVeterinary care intended to reduce or detect health risks, individualized according to the animal and local risks.
    SocializationThe developmental process through which an animal learns about people, animals, environments and experiences; safe approaches vary by species and individual.
    Parasite controlMeasures used to prevent or manage parasites; appropriate products and schedules depend on species, geography, lifestyle and veterinary guidance.
    PalatabilityHow appealing a food is to an animal in terms of taste, smell and texture.
    CarrierA secure transport enclosure designed for an animal's size and intended use.
    Questions

    Frequently asked questions.

    How much should I feed my pet?

    There is no universal portion. Species, food formulation, body size, age, activity, reproductive status and health all matter. Product feeding guides can be a starting reference, while a veterinarian can help assess body condition and individual needs.

    How much exercise does a dog need?

    Needs vary by age, breed/type, fitness, health, weather and individual temperament. Rather than relying on a universal number, choose activity appropriate to the individual and adjust based on veterinary advice where health limitations exist.

    Do indoor cats still need enrichment?

    Yes. Indoor environments can provide opportunities for climbing, hiding, scratching, hunting-style play, exploration and food-related activities. Preferences differ between cats.

    How often should I groom my pet?

    Coat type, shedding, skin condition, lifestyle and species affect grooming frequency. Long or easily matted coats generally need different care from short coats. Seek professional guidance if grooming causes pain or reveals skin problems.

    Can I give my pet human medicine?

    Do not give a human medication unless a veterinarian has specifically instructed you to use that medication for that animal. Some medicines that are common for people can be dangerous to pets.

    When should I call a veterinarian?

    Contact a veterinarian when you notice concerning or persistent changes in appetite, drinking, breathing, elimination, mobility, behavior, pain or general condition. Emergency signs such as collapse, breathing difficulty, severe trauma or suspected poisoning require prompt attention.
